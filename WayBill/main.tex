\title{\system: Privacy-Preserving Road Usage Charging from Authenticated Trajectory Settlement Proofs}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{[Author names removed for blind review]}
\IEEEauthorblockA{[Affiliation removed for blind review]}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Road usage charging needs to bill vehicles by where and how far they drive, but
straightforward GNSS upload turns the charging authority into a location
surveillance service. Existing privacy-preserving road-pricing systems protect
route information with commitments, anonymous credentials, or spot checks, but
their enforcement story still relies on roadside observation: a vehicle can be
forced to open a commitment only when external infrastructure sees it. This
couples privacy-preserving charging to city-scale cameras, RSUs, or patrol
coverage.

We present \system, a settlement protocol for zone-based road usage charging
that shifts enforcement from roadside observation to proof verification.
\system\ uses authenticated receiver fixes and odometer readings as the trusted
sensing layer, but keeps tariff policy, physical feasibility, period binding,
completeness reconciliation, and billing arithmetic outside the trusted
computing base. Each vehicle submits a proof-only public statement for a
period bill; the charger verifies that the bill is consistent with authenticated
private fixes, a committed tariff table, odometer distance, cadence and speed
rules, and a canonical billing month. A monthly odometer attestation then
reconciles any distance not covered by accepted period proofs, so withholding
an entire period becomes a self-pricing event rather than a free kilometer.

Across four mobility datasets, \system\ exposes the economic value of each
settlement constraint, quantifies proof-consistent relay residuals, and compares
proof rejection with reconstructed spot-check enforcement. The implementation
includes a 226k-constraint Groth16 settlement circuit, a proof-only charger
service with monthly reconciliation, and a receipted evaluation harness. The
remaining privacy claim is deliberately narrow: the charger does not see raw
routes, but the minimal public statement still leaks fee, distance, fallback,
and timing metadata; we quantify this leakage and evaluate bucketing and
selective-disclosure dispute openings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Road usage charging (\ruc) is becoming a practical replacement or supplement
for fuel taxes, congestion charges, and zone-based road pricing. Its technical
appeal is clear: a public authority can charge vehicles by distance, zone, time,
or vehicle class instead of relying on fuel consumption as a proxy. Its privacy
failure mode is equally clear: the simplest implementation uploads GNSS traces
or detailed trip records to the charging operator.

The classical privacy-preserving tolling literature recognized this tension
early. Systems such as VPriv, PrETP, Milo, and related cell-based road pricing
designs hide most of a driver's route, but they rely on spot checks or roadside
observations to enforce completeness: if a vehicle omits a charged segment, an
external observer must catch it and demand an opening.
\TODO{Cite Popa2009VPriv, Balasch2010PrETP, Meiklejohn2011Milo,
Garcia2013CellBased after bib verification.}
This is the historical reason for roadside cameras and RSUs in private
road-pricing systems. They buy completeness, not merely arithmetic correctness.

\system\ separates these two enforcement dimensions. Correctness is handled by
a settlement proof: the vehicle proves that an accepted period bill is computed
from authenticated fixes, odometer deltas, public tariff rates, and physical
constraints. Completeness is handled by monthly odometer reconciliation: any
attested distance not covered by accepted period statements is billed at a
fallback rate no lower than the maximum tariff rate. Missing fixes, missing
intervals, missing periods, and missing monthly attestations therefore form a
monotone degradation chain. Failure to report is not a privacy-preserving way to
pay less; it moves the vehicle to a fallback or administrative path.

This design point depends on trusted sensing, but it does not place pricing
policy in the trusted computing base (TCB). The receiver attests sensor readings
and odometer boundaries; it does not decide how much the user owes. Tariff
membership, period and month binding, physical feasibility, fallback treatment,
and bill arithmetic are verified by public statements, proofs, and charger-side
checks. We summarize the design thesis as follows:

\begin{quote}
\system\ is, to our knowledge, the first \ruc\ design point that jointly
achieves zone-based charging, route privacy, and zero roadside enforcement
infrastructure without placing pricing policy inside the trusted computing
base: the trusted device attests readings, never bills them.
\end{quote}

We make six contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Design point.} We identify the \ruc\ trilemma of zone pricing,
  route privacy, and zero roadside enforcement, and show how authenticated
  sensing plus policy-free proof verification dissolves it.
  \item \textbf{\epssec.} We introduce a quantitative economic security metric
  for verifiable billing and a receipt-auditable constraint-ablation
  methodology built around a shared billing oracle.
  \item \textbf{Incentive-compatible degradation.} We prove sufficient
  conditions for weakly dominant truthful reporting at every degradation layer,
  including per-interval outage and whole-period withholding under monthly
  odometer reconciliation.
  \item \textbf{Enforcement game.} We model spot-check enforcement as a
  Stackelberg game on real trajectories and compare deterministic proof
  rejection with placement-aware omission under partial camera coverage.
  \item \textbf{Settlement metadata leakage.} We quantify the side channel
  exposed by proof-only public statements, including bucketing mitigations and
  selective-disclosure dispute openings.
  \item \textbf{Artifact.} We implement a 226k-constraint Groth16 settlement
  circuit, a proof-only charger service with monthly reconciliation, and a
  receipted evaluation harness over four mobility datasets.
\end{itemize}

\section{System and Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

\subsection{Parties and Deployment Assumptions}

The system has three operational parties. A vehicle carries a receiver that
records authenticated GNSS fixes, odometer readings, nonces, and receiver
signatures. A charger maintains the device registry, tariff roots, verification
keys, accepted period statements, and monthly ledgers. A tariff authority
publishes the zone-rate table and tariff version.

The trusted sensing assumption is deliberately narrow. The receiver is trusted
to sign sensor readings and monthly odometer boundaries under an enrolled key.
It is not trusted to evaluate tariffs, choose fallback behavior, or compute the
final charge. \system\ consumes existing hardware-style assumptions such as
authenticated GNSS and tamper-evident odometry; it does not claim to solve
device-key extraction, hardware odometer rollback, or malicious enrollment.

\subsection{Adversary}

The adversarial driver wants to reduce payment while being accepted by the
charger. In scope are false zone claims, tariff-root substitution, period or
month rebinding, lifted replay, interval omission, whole-period withholding, and
proof-consistent signal-layer relay capabilities. Out of scope are full device
key compromise, undetectable physical odometer tampering, and enrollment of a
fake device as honest hardware.

\subsection{Security and Privacy Goals}

\paragraph{Billing correctness.}
If a period statement is accepted, the public fee, distance, fallback count,
period window, month window, and roots are bound to a witness trajectory that
satisfies the settlement relation.

\paragraph{Monthly revenue soundness.}
Every driven meter is either covered by an accepted period statement or charged
through monthly reconciliation. Whole-period withholding cannot reduce payment
when the reconciliation rate is at least the maximum tariff rate.

\paragraph{Policy-free TCB.}
Tariff policy and billing arithmetic are verified outside the receiver TCB.
Changing a tariff updates public data and proofs, not trusted billing firmware.

\paragraph{Bounded leakage.}
The proof-only charger view reveals a leakage profile
$L=(\mathrm{fee},\mathrm{distance},\mathrm{fallbacks},\mathrm{time/month},
\mathrm{roots})$ and the monthly odometer delta. It does not reveal raw fixes,
per-interval fees, or a zone histogram. We do not claim that $L$ is harmless;
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} measures its residual side channel.

\subsection{Period Statement}

Each period statement contains identifiers, timing fields, roots, and billing
totals:
\begin{align*}
\mathsf{stmt} = (&\mathsf{period\_id}, \mathsf{month\_id},
\mathsf{period\_start}, \mathsf{period\_end}, \\
&\mathsf{fee}, \mathsf{distance}, \mathsf{fallbacks},
\mathsf{fix\_root}, \mathsf{interval\_root}, \\
&\mathsf{tariff\_root}, \mathsf{tariff\_version},
\mathsf{device\_attestation}).
\end{align*}
The statement also carries a statement commitment, and the proof's public
signal vector binds the same values. The charger rejects any mismatch between
the proof signals, statement commitment, canonical month window, and registered
device attestation commitment.

\subsection{Billing Relation}

For each interval, the witness supplies two authenticated fixes. The circuit and
charger logic enforce time monotonicity, a maximum interval duration, a speed
budget on odometer delta, tariff membership under a committed tariff root, and
the fallback rule. Exact intervals are billed as odometer distance times the
current zone rate. Fallback intervals are billed as
$\lceil \Delta t \cdot v_{\max}\rceil$ times the fallback rate.

\subsection{Monthly Reconciliation}

Accepted period windows are keyed by $(\mathsf{device\_id},\mathsf{period\_id})$
and rejected if they overlap for the same device. At month end, the receiver
signs $(\mathsf{device\_id},\mathsf{month\_id},
\mathsf{odometer\_start},\mathsf{odometer\_end})$. The charger computes
\[
  d_{\mathrm{unacct}} =
  (\mathsf{odometer\_end} - \mathsf{odometer\_start}) -
  \sum_{\mathsf{accepted}} d_{\mathsf{period}} .
\]
If $d_{\mathrm{unacct}}\ge 0$, it charges this distance at the reconciliation
rate. If the monthly attestation is missing, the enrolled device-month appears
on an administrative queue rather than disappearing from the settlement system.
Adjacent monthly attestations must chain: the previous month end reading must
equal the current month start reading.

\begin{proposition}[Monthly revenue soundness]
\label{prop:monthly-soundness}
Assume receiver odometer attestations are unforgeable and chained across
adjacent months. If the reconciliation rate is at least the maximum tariff rate,
then the accepted monthly bill is at least the tariff-true fee of all driven
distance in the month, whether or not the driver submits all period proofs.
Truthful full submission is weakly dominant; it is strictly dominant for any
withheld distance whose realized tariff is strictly below the reconciliation
rate.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Every accepted period contributes its verified exact or fallback fee to the
ledger. For any driven distance not covered by accepted periods, monthly
reconciliation charges at a rate no lower than any tariff rate. Therefore
withholding a period cannot lower the charge for the withheld distance. Equality
can occur at the boundary where the realized tariff equals the reconciliation
rate, which is why the dominance statement is weak at $\alpha=1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Dispute Openings}

The interval commitment root also supports selective disclosure. In a dispute,
the vehicle opens only the challenged interval-level accounting tuple, and only
if necessary escalates to endpoint fix disclosure. The default arbitration mode
should be level-1 accounting disclosure, because level-2 endpoint openings have
measurable anonymity loss.

\section{Security and Leakage Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

\subsection{Billing Correctness}

Billing correctness follows from the settlement relation, public-signal binding,
and charger-side recomputation. A proof cannot be detached from a cheaper
statement because the public signal vector includes the roots, totals, tariff
version, device attestation field, maximum interval duration, period window, and
canonical month window. The raw-fix endpoint is an engineering validation path;
the privacy deployment path is proof-only and rejects payloads containing raw
fixes.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\epssec}{epsilon-economic soundness}}

We use \epssec\ to quantify residual billing attack surface. For an adversary
class $\mathcal{A}$, $\epsilon$ is the maximum accepted fractional bill reduction
relative to the canonical billing oracle:
\[
  \epsilon(\mathcal{A}) =
  \sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
  \frac{\mathsf{bill}_{\mathrm{honest}}-\mathsf{bill}_{a}}
       {\mathsf{bill}_{\mathrm{honest}}}.
\]
E1 estimates $\epsilon$ when individual defenses are removed. E3 estimates
proof-consistent residual $\epsilon$ under relay/meaconing capabilities. E2
establishes a sufficient zero-gain condition for outage and withholding
strategies.

\subsection{Leakage Profile}

The proof-only view is simulatable from the public leakage profile $L$ and the
registered device metadata. This is a privacy boundary, not a no-leakage claim.
The fee-distance-fallback-time tuple can fingerprint periods in a small
observed pool. Our evaluation therefore reports anonymity sets, bucketing
effects, and dispute-opening leakage.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Questions and Setup}

The evaluation answers six questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item E0: How accurate is honest billing under proof-compatible footprints?
  \item E1: Which defenses block which economic attack surface?
  \item E2: What fallback and reconciliation rates make withholding
  unprofitable, and what honest-user penalty do they impose?
  \item E3: How much economic residual remains under proof-consistent relay?
  \item E4: How much roadside observation does spot-check enforcement need to
  match deterministic proof rejection?
  \item E5/E6: What does the proof-only statement leak, and what is the proving
  cost?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{E0: Honest Billing Accuracy}

On zone-homogeneous footprints, the honest bill is exact. On the multi-zone
Beijing grid, the attribution error is two-sided with near-zero mean
(+0.26\%), median zero, and p95 absolute error 10.7\%. This should be presented
as quantization noise rather than systematic over- or under-charging.

\subsection{E2: Fallback and Reconciliation}

The sufficient bound is simple: if fallback and reconciliation rates are no
lower than the maximum tariff, withholding cannot reduce the bill. The empirical
frontier then locates less conservative operating points and reports the honest
penalty. The 2026-06-17 protocol recheck loaded 44 periods and rewrote the E2
frontier outputs under \texttt{gap\_protocol\_recheck\_20260617}.

\subsection{E3: Proof-Consistent Relay Residual}

Authenticated GNSS does not eliminate relay or meaconing capabilities. \system\
therefore treats signal-layer manipulation as residual economic exposure rather
than impossibility. The current full sweep reports a conservative worst p95
residual of 0.507 in the Rome fine/intra-cell/400m setting; tighter 50m distance
buckets lower the comparable point from 0.464 to 0.295, and a 60s cadence cuts
the worst Rome coarse-grid residual to 0.187.

\subsection{E4: Enforcement Exchange Rate}

We compare deterministic proof rejection with reconstructed spot-check
enforcement. Against a placement-aware omitter, any partial camera coverage can
yield zero detection if the omitted route avoids covered segments; closing the
evasion path requires full coverage, at which point honest route visibility is
also complete. On the central Beijing grid, the spot-check equivalent is about
17,000 roadside cameras versus zero for proof rejection.

\subsection{E5: Settlement Metadata Leakage}

The proof-only statement is not leakage-free. Exact per-period tuples can have
median anonymity one in small pools; bucketing improves anonymity monotonically.
In the current statement-bucketing output, Rome reaches median anonymity three
at a 50\% grid. Odometer-only mode reaches a higher anonymity set because it
removes zone-priced timing detail. A single level-2 dispute opening
de-anonymizes 76\% of periods, so level-1 openings should be the default.

\subsection{E6: Performance and Artifact}

The sealed circuit profile has 21 public signals and about 226k constraints.
The measured proof cost is 1.049s with rapidsnark and 14.28s with snarkjs for a
2h period; at 60s cadence a full month costs about 157s of background client
CPU. Verification costs 242--276ms and proof communication is below 1KB for the
binary proof, with JSON/public-signal overhead reported separately.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Trusted sensing remains necessary.}
\system\ does not remove the need for an enrolled, tamper-evident sensing layer.
It reduces what that layer is trusted to do. Device compromise and undetectable
odometer tampering remain outside the cryptographic settlement guarantee.

\paragraph{Public metadata leakage is real.}
The proof-only statement intentionally exposes a small billing tuple. This is
enough for useful side-channel analysis in small pools. Deferred-disclosure
running-total commitments are a natural v6 branch, but they are not part of the
sealed artifact.

\paragraph{Cross-domain linkability.}
The charger must link a device within a billing month to collect payment.
Cross-charger or cross-domain linkability can be reduced with per-domain
pseudonymous attestation keys, which we leave to deployment work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\system\ shows that private zone-based road usage charging can separate
trusted sensing from trusted pricing. Settlement proofs handle arithmetic and
physical feasibility; monthly odometer reconciliation handles completeness; and
the charger learns a bounded public statement rather than a route. The resulting
design replaces roadside observation with proof verification while making its
residual assumptions and leakage explicit.
